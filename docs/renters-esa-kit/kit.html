012026
What changed in 2026

May 22, 2026
changed the process,
not the law.

HUD's Office of Fair Housing issued a memo that withdrew its earlier ESA guidance and changed which housing complaints it will pursue. The Fair Housing Act itself was not rewritten.

What changed

  • HUD withdrew its 2020 guidance on assistance animals, which had treated ESAs as assistance animals rather than pets.
  • HUD now focuses enforcement on animals individually trained to do disability-related work or tasks.
  • Landlords feel freer to ask questions and decide requests for untrained ESAs case by case.
  • HUD says it plans a formal rulemaking, with public comment, to update its assistance animal rules.

What did not change

  • The Fair Housing Act and its reasonable accommodation requirement are still law.
  • You can still make a written request and ask your landlord to consider it.
  • State and local fair housing laws still apply, and some protect ESAs on their own.
  • A private lawsuit under the Fair Housing Act remains possible.
What this means for you: a clear written request, a solid letter and a good paper trail matter more in 2026 than they did before.

Sources: HUD FHEO enforcement memo of May 22, 2026, as summarized by the National Association of Realtors, Baker Donelson and the Disability Rights Education and Defense Fund. Check for updates, as HUD has said new rules are coming.

02LETTER
Is your letter landlord-ready?

Seven marks of
a solid ESA letter.

Landlords look harder at letters now. Hold yours next to this sample and check each numbered mark.

1Jordan Avery, LCSWLicensed Clinical Social Worker
2License #00000 · TexasPhone · Email · Address
SAMPLE

3March 4, 2026

To whom it may concern,

Sam Rivera is my client. I have evaluated Sam and am familiar with Sam's history and functional limitations. 4Sam has a mental health condition that substantially limits one or more major life activities.

5Sam's emotional support animal, a dog named Rex, provides support that alleviates one or more symptoms of this condition. I recommend that Sam be allowed to keep Rex in their home as a reasonable accommodation.

Please contact me with any questions about this letter.

6Jordan Avery

Jordan Avery, LCSW

  • 1A licensed professional, such as a therapist, counselor, clinical social worker, psychologist or psychiatrist.
  • 2Licensed in your state, with license type, number and state, so it can be checked.
  • 3Recent date. Many landlords expect a letter dated within the last 12 months.
  • 4A disability-related need. It does not have to name your diagnosis.
  • 5Your animal, named or described, so it is clear which animal the request covers.
  • 6Signed by the professional, on letterhead with contact details.
  • 7From a real evaluation. The professional spoke with you before writing it.

Red flags that get letters questioned

  • "Registration" numbers or certificates
  • No license number or state
  • Issued instantly, with no conversation
  • A generic letter with no animal named
  • Older than a year
  • Professional not licensed where you live

03REQUEST
The request template

Fill in the blanks.
Send it in writing.

Email works well because it is dated. Attach your ESA letter and keep a copy of everything you send.

Subject: Reasonable accommodation request for unit / address

Dear landlord or property manager name,

I live at address and unit (or I am applying to rent it). I am writing to request a reasonable accommodation for a disability under the Fair Housing Act and applicable state law.

I am asking to keep my emotional support animal, animal's name, a species and breed, in my home. I have attached a letter from professional's name and license confirming my disability-related need for this animal.

I will keep animal's name under control, clean up after them, and take responsibility for any damage they cause. If you need any other information to review this request, please let me know in writing.

I would appreciate a written reply by date, about 10 days out. Thank you for considering my request.

Sincerely,
your name · phone · date

Keep it shortYou don't need to share your diagnosis or medical history.
Send it earlyBefore you sign a lease or before the animal arrives, when you can.
Stay politeA calm, simple request is easier to say yes to.

04SCRIPTS
When your landlord asks questions

Calm answers to
common questions.

Questions are normal, especially after the 2026 change. Answer in writing where you can, and keep it brief.

THEY ASK"What is your diagnosis?"
You generally don't have to share it. Your letter confirms a disability-related need."My letter from a licensed professional confirms my disability-related need. I'd prefer to keep my medical details private. Is there anything else you need to review it?"
THEY ASK"Can I verify your letter?"
Yes. Landlords can check that the professional is licensed. That is one reason a license number matters."Of course. The professional's license number and contact details are on the letter."
THEY SAY"We don't allow pets."
A no-pets rule is the reason you are asking for an accommodation, not a reason to skip considering it."I understand. I'm asking for an exception as a disability accommodation. Could you review my request and reply in writing?"
THEY SAY"There's a pet fee or deposit."
Since May 2026 this depends more on your state and local law. Ask for the policy in writing and check your state's rules."Could you send me the fee policy in writing? I'd like to understand how it applies to an accommodation request."
THEY ASK"Is your animal trained?"
ESAs don't need task training, but basic manners help. Describe how you'll manage your animal."Rex is house-trained, quiet and stays with me or crated. I'll be responsible for any damage."

If you're refused
  1. Ask for the reason in writing, and whether another arrangement would work for them.
  2. Check your state and city fair housing laws. Some protect ESAs more than federal rules now do.
  3. Contact your state or local fair housing agency or a legal aid office for advice.
  4. Watch the deadlines. HUD complaints are generally due within one year, and private Fair Housing Act lawsuits within two years.
