01 · What changed in 2026
May 22,2026.
The day HUD changed how it handles ESA housing complaints. The law stayed. The process moved.

What changed

  • HUD withdrew its 2020 assistance animal guidance, which had treated ESAs as assistance animals rather than pets.
  • It now focuses enforcement on animals individually trained to do disability-related tasks.
  • Landlords feel freer to ask questions and decide untrained-ESA requests case by case.
  • HUD plans a formal rulemaking, with public comment, to update its rules.

What did not change

  • The Fair Housing Act and its reasonable accommodation requirement are still law.
  • You can still make a written request and ask your landlord to consider it.
  • State and local fair housing laws still apply, and some protect ESAs on their own.
  • A private lawsuit under the Fair Housing Act remains possible.

For renters, that means a clear written request, a solid letter and a good paper trail now do more of the work.

02 · The letter check

Seven marks.
Check every one.

Landlords look harder at letters now. Hold yours next to this list before you send it.

  1. Written by a licensed professionalA therapist, counselor, clinical social worker, psychologist or psychiatrist.
  2. Licensed in your stateLicense type, number and issuing state, so it can be checked.
  3. On letterhead, signed and datedWith contact details so your landlord can verify it.
  4. RecentMany landlords expect a letter dated within the last 12 months.
  5. A disability-related needIt confirms the need. It does not have to name your diagnosis.
  6. Your animal, namedSo it is clear which animal the request covers.
  7. From a real evaluationThe professional spoke with you before writing it.

Red flags that get letters questioned

  • "Registration" numbers, IDs or certificates
  • No license number or state
  • Issued instantly, with no conversation
  • No animal named
  • More than a year old
  • Professional not licensed where you live

03 · Your request

Fill it in.
Send it in writing.

Email works well because it is dated. Attach your ESA letter and keep a copy of everything.

Subject: Reasonable accommodation request for unit / address

Dear landlord or manager,

I live at address and unit (or I am applying to rent it). I am writing to request a reasonable accommodation for a disability under the Fair Housing Act and applicable state law.

I am asking to keep my emotional support animal, animal's name, a species and breed, in my home. I have attached a letter from professional's name and license confirming my disability-related need for this animal.

I will keep animal's name under control, clean up after them, and take responsibility for any damage. If you need anything else to review this request, please let me know in writing.

I would appreciate a written reply by date, about 10 days out. Thank you for considering my request.

Sincerely,
your name   phone   date

04 · When they ask

Five questions,
five calm answers.

Questions are normal after the 2026 change. Answer in writing where you can, and keep it brief.

THEY ASK"What is your diagnosis?"
You generally don't have to share it. Your letter confirms the need."My letter confirms my disability-related need. I'd like to keep medical details private. Is there anything else you need to review it?"
THEY ASK"Can I verify your letter?"
Yes. Landlords can check that the professional is licensed."Of course. The license number and contact details are on the letter."
THEY SAY"We don't allow pets."
A no-pets rule is why you're asking for an accommodation, not a reason to skip considering it."I understand. I'm asking for an exception as a disability accommodation. Could you review it and reply in writing?"
THEY SAY"There's a pet fee."
Since May 2026 this depends more on your state and local law."Could you send me the fee policy in writing, so I can see how it applies to an accommodation?"
THEY ASK"Is your animal trained?"
ESAs don't need task training, but good manners help your case."Rex is house-trained and quiet, and I'll be responsible for any damage."

If you're refused

  1. Ask why, in writing, and whether another arrangement would work.
  2. Check state and city law. Some protect ESAs more than federal rules now do.
  3. Call a fair housing agency or legal aid office for advice.
